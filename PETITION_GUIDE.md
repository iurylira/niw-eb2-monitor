# NIW Approval Guide — Steps to Improve Your Odds

Built from the patterns in `REPORT.md` (1224 AAO NIW appeal decisions, Jan 2025–Jul 2026) plus the official adjudication guidance USCIS actually applies. This is not legal advice — it's a checklist to walk into an attorney review already informed, and to self-audit a draft before you file.

**Read this caveat first:** the dataset behind this guide is AAO *appeals* — cases that already lost once at USCIS and got contested. It tells you what reliably sinks a case that reaches appeal. It does not tell you the overall first-instance approval rate, which is presumably much higher. Treat every number below as "what to avoid," not "what your odds are."

---

## Step 0 — Read the primary source before drafting anything

- [USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 6, Part F, Ch. 5](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-6-part-f-chapter-5) — the guidance officers actually adjudicate against.
- [USCIS Jan 15, 2025 policy update](https://www.uscis.gov/sites/default/files/document/policy-manual-updates/20250115-Employment-BasedNationalInterestWaivers.pdf) — applies to anything pending or filed on/after that date. Almost the entire dataset behind this guide falls under it, so the patterns below reflect *this* version of the guidance, not the pre-2025 one.

Everything after this step assumes you've read those two documents once. This guide tells you where petitioners actually fail against that guidance — it doesn't restate the guidance itself.

---

## Step 1 — Define the endeavor narrowly, not the field

**Why this is step 1:** Prong 1 (national importance) is the dispositive issue in 74.8% of all denials in the dataset — by far the single biggest lever you control. Within Prong 1, the most common specific trap is `P1_FIELD_VS_ENDEAVOR_CONFLATION` (15.8% of all cases): arguing why the *profession* matters ("AI is important," "nursing is important") instead of why *your specific proposed work* matters.

**Do this:**
- Write one sentence naming your endeavor that a stranger outside your field could repeat back accurately. If it reads like a job title or an industry description, it's still the field, not the endeavor.
- Every importance claim must trace back to *this* endeavor's output — not to your employer's product line, not to your industry's growth statistics in general.

**Don't do this:**
- Don't lean on labor-shortage or Schedule A arguments as your main case — rejected in 7.4% of all decisions (`P1_SHORTAGE_ARGUMENT_REJECTED`) when used as the primary basis.
- Don't lean on depressed-area / HUBZone / opportunity-zone framing — rejected in 8.4% of cases (`P1_DEPRESSED_AREA_ARGUMENT_REJECTED`).
- If you cite job creation or economic impact numbers, show the methodology. Unsupported projections fail in 7.0% of cases (`P1_ECONOMIC_PROJECTIONS_UNSUPPORTED`) — a number with no basis is worse than no number.

## Step 2 — Choose your endeavor framing deliberately

The dataset shows framing correlates strongly with outcome:

| Endeavor type | Sample size | Sustained on appeal |
|---|---|---|
| `researcher_academic` | 131 | 9.2% |
| `employed_professional` | 823 | 1.1% |
| `entrepreneur_business_plan` | 172 | **0.0%** |

If your work can honestly be framed as research/academic — tied to publications, grants, named initiatives, or institutional collaboration — that framing has historically fared far better than a generic business-plan pitch. This isn't about disguising what you do; it's about which true framing of your endeavor you lead with. If you're building a company, the AAO's skepticism toward business-plan endeavors (0/172 sustained in this dataset) means that argument needs to be airtight *before* it ever reaches appeal — don't count on the appeal stage to save it.

`business_entrepreneurship_management`, `legal`, `education_academia`, and `finance_accounting` occupation categories all show 0% sustained in this dataset. That doesn't mean don't file — it means those categories carry a heavier burden on Prong 1 specificity and should get extra scrutiny before submission.

## Step 3 — Prove you're positioned to pull it off (Prong 2)

Second-biggest lever: `P2_POSITIONING_INSUFFICIENT` appears in 29.2% of denials, and it frequently fails *alongside* Prong 1 — a weak endeavor definition and weak positioning tend to travel together.

**Do this:**
- Write a concrete, milestoned plan for the next 1–3 years: what you'll do, with whom, funded how. `P2_PLAN_VAGUE_OR_NOT_ACTIONABLE` (6.0% of cases) is a distinct, avoidable failure — "I will continue advancing this field" is not a plan.
- Make your record of past achievement *probative*, not just impressive. `P2_PAST_ACHIEVEMENTS_NOT_PROBATIVE` (2.9%) means the AAO wants achievements that demonstrably predict success at *this* endeavor, not a generic CV highlight reel.

---

## Step 4 — Prong 3: don't over-invest, but don't phone it in either

The balance-of-factors prong (whether the U.S. benefits more from waiving the labor cert than not) is cited in 12.7% of denials but is the *sole* dispositive reason in only 6 of 1224 cases (0.5%). It's a tie-breaker on an already-strong case, not where marginal drafting time pays off best — Steps 1–3 come first. But it still needs to be done correctly, since `P3_BALANCE_NOT_IN_US_FAVOR` shows up as a contributing factor in 155 cases. ([Source](https://www.colombohurdlaw.com/visas/eb-2-national-interest-waiver-niw/in-depth-look-at-the-third-prong-of-dhanasar-beneficial-to-the-united-states/))

**The actual question** is not "would harm come from denying the waiver" — it's "on balance, would it benefit the U.S. to skip the job offer and labor certification process for this person." You don't need to show the process would harm you; you need to affirmatively show why skipping it is the better outcome for the U.S.

**The three factors USCIS weighs:**
1. **Impracticality of labor certification** — the standard PERM process doesn't fit your situation (e.g., self-employed, no fixed employer, a role that doesn't map to a standard occupation code).
2. **Unique contribution despite available domestic workers** — you bring value beyond what the labor market test would surface, even if workers in your field exist.
3. **Sufficiently urgent national interest** — a national need (STEM gaps, public health, economic competitiveness) urgent enough to justify skipping the standard process's delay.

**Common mistakes to avoid here specifically:**
- Restating your Prong 1/2 achievements without connecting them explicitly to *why skipping labor cert benefits the U.S.* — impact alone doesn't satisfy Prong 3; the connection has to be made explicit.
- If you're an entrepreneur: merely running a business isn't enough — show specific job creation or an unmet market need you address, not just that the business exists.
- Arguing about the harms or burdens of the labor-cert process itself, instead of affirmatively demonstrating the benefit of waiving it — this is a negative-framing trap the guidance specifically warns against.
- Citing industry statistics or shortage data with no bridge back to how *you specifically* address the gap — general statistics need a specific, personal tie-in.

**Structure that works:** open with the national-level context (credible statistics, government reports), then explicitly bridge to your specific contribution — how your work addresses the identified gap — and back it with concrete evidence: revenue, job-creation records, patents, research adoption, industry partnerships. Acknowledging the value of labor protections while showing why your case is the exception tends to read as more credible than treating the requirement as an obstacle to argue around.

## Step 5 — Make the evidence itself bulletproof

These are execution problems, fully within your control regardless of how strong the underlying endeavor is:

- **Corroboration.** `EVIDENCE_INSUFFICIENT_CORROBORATION` fails 12.7% of cases — assertions without supporting proof. Every claim needs a document, data point, or citation behind it, not just a statement in the personal statement or a letter.
- **Reference letters.** `EVIDENCE_GENERIC_LETTERS` fails 5.1% of cases — letters that read as generic praise ("Dr. X is an excellent researcher") rather than specific, probative testimony tied to your actual endeavor and its importance. A good letter states *what* you did, *why it mattered*, and *how the writer knows*.

---

## Step 6 — Front-load everything; don't save your best evidence for appeal

This is the single most preventable mistake in the whole dataset. `PROCEDURAL_NEW_EVIDENCE_ON_APPEAL` shows up in 106 cases (8.7%) — and of those, only **1** was sustained. 92 were dismissed outright; the AAO routinely declines to consider evidence introduced for the first time on appeal.

**Practical implication:** treat the initial I-140 filing (and any RFE response) as your only real shot at submitting evidence. The appeal is for arguing the officer made a *legal* error on the record that existed — it is not a second chance to submit the proof you didn't have the first time. If you're not sure a piece of evidence is ready, get it ready before filing, not after a denial.

---

## Step 7 — Get a second read before submitting

- Search your own occupation/field directly against the [AAO Non-Precedent Decisions database](https://www.uscis.gov/administrative-appeals/aao-decisions/aao-non-precedent-decisions) — find decisions closest to your fact pattern and read what specifically sank or saved them.
- Consider a second opinion from an open-source comparison tool: [JP2670/eb2NIW](https://github.com/JP2670/eb2NIW) compares petition drafts against 7,800+ AAO decisions (requires an OpenAI API key, costs money to run). [VeraSuperHub/vera-eb-suite](https://github.com/VeraSuperHub/vera-eb-suite) offers Claude Code skills for evidence review and pre-filing checks. Both are third-party, unverified in quality — useful as an extra pass, not a substitute for the next step.
- **Have an immigration attorney review the petition before filing.** Every recommendation in this guide is a pattern from appellate data, not a guarantee — the actual adjudication turns on how your specific facts map to the specific prong language, and that judgment call is what an attorney is for.

---

## Pre-filing self-check

Before you file, you should be able to answer "yes" to all of these:

- [ ] Can I state my endeavor in one sentence that isn't just my job title or field?
- [ ] Does every national-importance claim trace to *this* endeavor, not my employer/field generally?
- [ ] If I cite economic impact or job creation numbers, do I show the methodology?
- [ ] Have I avoided leaning primarily on labor-shortage or depressed-area arguments?
- [ ] Do I have a concrete, milestoned plan for the next 1–3 years, not just a statement of intent?
- [ ] Is every reference letter specific to my endeavor, not generic praise?
- [ ] Is every factual claim backed by a document or data point, not just an assertion?
- [ ] Have I included *everything* I have — not planning to hold anything back for a possible RFE or appeal?
- [ ] Has an immigration attorney reviewed the final draft?
